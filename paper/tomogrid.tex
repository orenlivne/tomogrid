\begin{abstract}
The forward operator of emission tomography is the attenuated Radon transform,
in which the attenuation enters through an exponential whose argument varies
along the line of integration. Evaluating it on an $n\times n$ grid for
$\bigO(n)$ directions costs $\bigO(n^3)=\bigO(N^{3/2})$ operations by direct
quadrature, where $N=n^2$, and this evaluation is the inner loop of every
iterative reconstruction method. We give an $\bigO(N\log N)$ algorithm in the
multilevel integral-transform framework of Brandt and Lubrecht. The algorithm
rests on an exact concatenation identity for a triple of segment integrals,
which lets the length of the integration segment double from level to level
with no approximation, and on the observation that a segment of length $\ell$
resolves direction only to $\delta\theta\sim h/\ell$, so that short segments
require few directions. Angular resolution is therefore doubled at the finest
levels in step with the segment length and the coarsest levels perform exact
merges only. We identify the quantities that must be interpolated --- the
attenuation exponent rather than its exponential, and a segment integral
referenced to its own exit point rather than to its midpoint --- and show that
the alternatives are unstable. Numerical experiments on a battery of
attenuation and activity phantoms confirm the predicted cost and show that the
error introduced by the multilevel schedule is an order of magnitude below the
discretisation error of the underlying grid by a factor of six to twelve, at a
cost already $2.9\times$ lower in operations and $4.5\times$ lower in wall time
at $n=129$, with the ratio falling as $\bigO(\log N/\sqrt{N})$.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The forward model}

Emission tomography reconstructs the spatial distribution of a radiotracer
from counts of the photons it emits. Two fields are involved: the activity
$f(\bx)\ge 0$, which is the unknown, and the linear attenuation coefficient
$\mu(\bx)\ge 0$ of the surrounding medium at the photon energy, which is
usually measured separately by a transmission or CT scan.

Parameterise a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$ by a direction and a signed offset,
\begin{equation}
  \om(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),\qquad
  \omp(\theta)=(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta),\qquad
  \bx(s,t)=s\,\omp+t\,\om ,
\end{equation}
and traverse $L(\theta,s)=\{\bx(s,t):t\in\mathbb{R}\}$ in the direction of
increasing $t$. A photon emitted at $\bx(s,t)$ and travelling along $\om$
escapes with probability $\exp(-\int_t^{\infty}\mu)$, so the expected count
along $L(\theta,s)$ is
\begin{equation}
  (\Rmu f)(\theta,s)
  = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f\big(\bx(s,t)\big)\,
    \exp\!\left(-\int_t^{\infty}\mu\big(\bx(s,\tau)\big)\,d\tau\right) dt .
  \label{eq:art}
\end{equation}
This is the attenuated Radon transform, also called the attenuated X-ray
transform. Its distinguishing feature is that the exponent depends on the
emission point $t$, so the attenuation does not factor out of the integral.
Equation~\eqref{eq:art} is the forward model of single-photon emission
computed tomography (SPECT).

Positron emission tomography (PET) detects two collinear annihilation photons
in coincidence. Both must escape, in opposite directions, so the survival
probability is $\exp(-\int_t^{\infty}\mu)\exp(-\int_{-\infty}^{t}\mu)
=\exp(-\int_L\mu)$, independent of $t$, and
\begin{equation}
  (\mathcal{P}_{\mu}f)(\theta,s)
  = \exp\!\left(-\int_L \mu\right)\int_L f .
  \label{eq:pet}
\end{equation}
Classical PET attenuation is thus a multiplicative factor per line. The
algorithm below computes the three line integrals
\begin{equation}
  S=\int_L f,\qquad E=\int_L\mu,\qquad I=(\Rmu f)(\theta,s),
  \label{eq:three}
\end{equation}
and therefore delivers both models: \eqref{eq:art} is $I$, and \eqref{eq:pet}
is $e^{-E}S$. The exponent is genuinely position-dependent only in
\eqref{eq:art}, and that is the case treated here; \eqref{eq:pet} is recovered
as a by-product at no extra cost. Time-of-flight PET, in which the coincidence
is localised along the line and the two one-sided attenuations no longer
combine into a constant, has the structure of \eqref{eq:art} and is covered by
the same machinery.

\subsection{The inverse problem and where evaluation enters}
\label{sec:motivation}

With $\mu$ known, \eqref{eq:art} is a linear Fredholm integral equation of the
first kind for $f$,
\begin{equation}
  \Rmu f = g ,
  \label{eq:inverse}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is the measured sinogram. Novikov \citep{novikov2002} gave an
explicit inversion formula for $\Rmu$, and for the unattenuated case
$\mu\equiv 0$ the classical filtered-backprojection inversion of the Radon
transform applies \citep{natterer2001}. In practice \eqref{eq:inverse} is
solved iteratively rather than by an inversion formula, because the data are
Poisson counts, because the system is large and ill-conditioned, and because
regularisation and physical corrections are easier to impose inside an
iteration.

The standard methods are all of this kind. The expectation-maximisation
algorithm for the Poisson likelihood \citep{sheppvardi1982} updates
\begin{equation}
  f^{(k+1)} = \frac{f^{(k)}}{\Rmu^{*}\mathbf{1}}\;
              \Rmu^{*}\!\left(\frac{g}{\Rmu f^{(k)}}\right),
  \label{eq:mlem}
\end{equation}
a multiplicative --- hence nonlinear --- relaxation; its ordered-subset
variant \citep{hudsonlarkin1994} applies \eqref{eq:mlem} over subsets of
directions in sequence, which makes it a block row-action scheme in the family
of algebraic reconstruction techniques \citep{gordon1970}, i.e.\ a Kaczmarz
relaxation with a nonlinear update. Every sweep of every one of these methods
applies $\Rmu$ once and its adjoint once. The cost of the reconstruction is
therefore a small multiple of the cost of evaluating $\Rmu$, and an
$\bigO(N^{3/2})$ evaluation caps the resolution that is reachable.

Two further considerations motivate a multilevel evaluation specifically
rather than any fast evaluation. First, if $\mu$ is not measured separately
but estimated jointly with $f$ from the emission data, the problem becomes
nonlinear in $\mu$, since $\mu$ sits in the exponent; the natural solver is
then a full approximation scheme (FAS) \citep{brandt1977} in which the
nonlinearity is handled on a hierarchy of grids. Second, a row-action
relaxation of \eqref{eq:inverse} is a smoother in the multigrid sense: it
reduces the components of the residual that the fine grid resolves, and leaves
the smooth components to be handled on coarser grids, so a full multigrid
(FMG) cycle over the image hierarchy is the natural outer solver. The level
hierarchy constructed here --- segment lengths paired with angular resolutions
--- is the same hierarchy such a solver would traverse, which is what makes the
evaluation and the solver composable. We do not develop the solver in this
paper. The present contribution is the forward evaluation; the adjoint, the
nonlinear inversion and the FAS/FMG cycle are the subject of subsequent work
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{Related work and contribution}

Fast summation by separating a smooth kernel on a hierarchy of grids is the
subject of the multilevel matrix-multiplication framework of Brandt and
Lubrecht \citep{brandtlubrecht1990} and its extension to oscillatory kernels
\citep{brandt1991}; the fast multipole method \citep{greengardrokhlin1987}
solves the same problem by a different decomposition. For line integrals
specifically, Brandt and Dym \citep{brandtdym1999} gave an $\bigO(N\log N)$
algorithm based on recursive doubling of segment length, and Brandt et al.\
\citep{brandtmann2000} built a multilevel inversion of the unattenuated Radon
transform on it. Fast backprojection by hierarchical decomposition, at
$\bigO(N\log^2 N)$ \citep{basubresler2000}, and transforms built on the
pseudopolar grid \citep{averbuch2008} achieve comparable asymptotics for the
unattenuated transform.

The contribution here is the extension to the attenuated transform
\eqref{eq:art}, where the smoothness of the exponent and of the integrand must
be exploited together. Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}
\item an exact concatenation identity (Proposition~\ref{prop:merge}) for a
  triple of segment integrals, which makes the doubling of segment length
  exact and, in addition, damping: an error committed upstream is multiplied by
  $e^{-E}\le 1$ at every merge;
\item identification of the quantities that admit interpolation --- the
  exponent $E$ rather than $e^{-E}$, and an exit-referenced rather than
  midpoint-referenced attenuated integral --- with the failure mode of each
  alternative;
\item a sampling analysis of the three axes (direction, transverse offset,
  position along the ray) which fixes the level schedule and shows that the
  angular refinement must be placed at the finest levels, not the coarsest;
\item a measured account of the resulting accuracy and cost, including the
  finding that the error budget is dominated not by the angular interpolation
  the method is built around, but by the resampling of the tables in the
  transverse and along-ray variables when the ray frame rotates.
\end{enumerate}
An open-source reference implementation, together with the scripts that
produce every number and figure below, is available
as~\citep{tomogrid}.

\section{Segment integrals and the concatenation identity}
\label{sec:identity}

The algorithm works with integrals over finite segments of a ray rather than
over whole lines. Fix $(\theta,s)$ and write $f(t)$, $\mu(t)$ for the
restrictions to that ray. For $a<b$ define
\begin{equation}
  E[a,b]=\int_a^b\mu(\tau)\,d\tau,\qquad
  S[a,b]=\int_a^b f(t)\,dt,\qquad
  I[a,b]=\int_a^b f(t)\,e^{-\int_t^b\mu(\tau)d\tau}\,dt .
  \label{eq:triple}
\end{equation}
The attenuation in $I$ is referenced to the segment's own exit point $b$, so
every weight lies in $(0,1]$. This choice is what makes the recursion stable
(Remark~\ref{rem:reference}).

\begin{proposition}[Concatenation]
\label{prop:merge}
For any $c\in(a,b)$,
\begin{equation}
  E[a,b]=E[a,c]+E[c,b],\qquad
  S[a,b]=S[a,c]+S[c,b],\qquad
  I[a,b]=e^{-E[c,b]}\,I[a,c]+I[c,b].
  \label{eq:merge}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The first two are additivity of the integral. For the third, split at $c$. For
$t\in[a,c]$ write $\int_t^b\mu=\int_t^c\mu+\int_c^b\mu$; the second term does
not depend on $t$ and factors out as $e^{-E[c,b]}$, leaving $I[a,c]$. For
$t\in[c,b]$ the integrand is unchanged and contributes $I[c,b]$.
\end{proof}

Equivalently, the lower-triangular matrices
\begin{equation}
  M[a,b]=\begin{pmatrix} e^{-E[a,b]} & 0\\ I[a,b] & 1\end{pmatrix}
  \qquad\text{satisfy}\qquad
  M[a,b]=M[a,c]\,M[c,b],
\end{equation}
so the triple $(S,E,I)$ carries a semigroup structure under concatenation.
Identity \eqref{eq:merge} involves no quadrature and no interpolation: given
the triples of two adjacent segments, the triple of their union is obtained
exactly. This is what permits the segment length to double from level to level
at no cost in accuracy.

\begin{remark}[Why $I$ is referenced to the exit point]
\label{rem:reference}
Referencing the attenuation to the segment midpoint $m$, i.e.\ using
$\tilde I[a,b]=\int_a^b f(t)e^{-\int_t^m\mu}dt$, gives weights as large as
$e^{+E[a,m]}$, which grow without bound with segment length and attenuation
strength. With the exit-referenced definition \eqref{eq:triple} all weights are
in $(0,1]$, and \eqref{eq:merge} shows that an error $\varepsilon$ in $I[a,c]$
propagates into $I[a,b]$ as $e^{-E[c,b]}\varepsilon$. Strong attenuation
therefore improves the conditioning of the recursion. This is confirmed in
Section~\ref{sec:battery}: the phantom with the largest attenuation gives the
smallest error.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Why $E$ and not $e^{-E}$ is interpolated]
\label{rem:exponent}
$E$ is an additive line integral of $\mu$, smoothed along the ray by the
averaging in \eqref{eq:triple}, with derivatives bounded in terms of those of
$\mu$. Its exponential spans many orders of magnitude --- in the experiments
below $E$ reaches $12$, so $e^{-E}\approx 6\times 10^{-6}$ --- and has
correspondingly large relative derivatives. The algorithm interpolates $E$ and
applies the exponential afterwards. This is the sense in which smoothness is
used twice: once in the exponent, through $E$, and once in the integrand,
through $I$.
\end{remark}

\section{Smoothness and sampling}
\label{sec:sampling}

Let $Q(\theta)$ denote a segment integral of length $\ell$ centred at a fixed
point $\bx$, as a function of the segment's direction. For a single Fourier
mode $f=e^{i\bk\cdot\mathbf{y}}$ with $|\bk|=k$,
\begin{equation}
  Q(\theta)=\int_{-\ell/2}^{\ell/2} e^{i\bk\cdot(\bx+\tau\om(\theta))}d\tau
  = e^{i\bk\cdot\bx}\,\ell\,
    \operatorname{sinc}\!\left(\tfrac{\ell k}{2}\cos(\theta-\phi)\right),
  \qquad \phi=\arg\bk .
  \label{eq:sinc}
\end{equation}
The $\theta$-dependence enters only through $u(\theta)=\frac{\ell k}{2}
\cos(\theta-\phi)$, with $|du/d\theta|\le \ell k/2$, and $\operatorname{sinc}$
varies on unit scale in $u$. Hence $Q$ varies in $\theta$ on the scale
$\delta\theta\sim 2/(\ell k)$. With the finest scale representable on a grid of
spacing $h$ being $k_{\max}\sim\pi/h$, the angular sampling requirement is
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;\delta\theta_\ell\,\ell \lesssim c\,h,\qquad c=\bigO(1).\;}
  \label{eq:angcrit}
\end{equation}
Geometrically \eqref{eq:angcrit} says that the endpoints of the segment must
not move by more than about one grid cell between neighbouring directions.
Short segments need few directions.

The other two axes follow from the same expression. Transverse to the ray no
averaging takes place, so the sample spacing $\delta s$ must resolve $f$
itself. Along the ray, $Q$ is a box average of length $\ell$, whose transfer
function is $\operatorname{sinc}(k\ell/2)$; this is not a strong low-pass
filter, since at the Nyquist frequency $k=\pi/\ell$ of a grid with
$\delta t=\ell$ it still equals $2/\pi\approx0.64$. Sampling the tables at
$\delta t=\ell$ therefore aliases. Taking
\begin{equation}
  \delta t=\ell/\nu,\qquad \nu=2,
  \label{eq:tcrit}
\end{equation}
places the grid Nyquist frequency exactly at the first zero of
$\operatorname{sinc}(k\ell/2)$. Section~\ref{sec:knobs} confirms that $\nu=2$
is both necessary and sufficient: $\nu=4$ and $\nu=8$ give no further
improvement. Segments at a given level consequently overlap by half.

\section{The algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}

\subsection{Hierarchy}

Let the support of $f$ and $\mu$ lie in the disc of radius $R$ and let
$H>R$ be a half-chord that covers any line of interest. Levels
$l=0,\dots,L$ carry segments of length
\begin{equation}
  \ell_l = 2^{\,l}\ell_0,\qquad \ell_0\approx h,\qquad \ell_L = 2H .
\end{equation}
Level $l$ stores, for each direction $\theta_k$ on a grid $\Theta_l$, a table
of the triple \eqref{eq:triple} over $(s,t)$, where $t$ is the segment centre.
The grids are
\begin{equation}
  \delta s = h/\sigma,\qquad
  \delta t_l = \ell_l/\nu,\qquad
  |\Theta_l| = \min\big(n_\theta,\; n_\theta^{\min}2^{\,l}\big),
  \label{eq:schedule}
\end{equation}
with $\Theta_l=\{2\pi i/|\Theta_l| \}$ so that $\Theta_{l-1}\subset\Theta_l$,
and $t$-grids $t_j=-H+j\,\delta t_l$ so that the level-$l$ grid is every second
node of the level-$(l-1)$ grid.

The last entry of \eqref{eq:schedule} is the scheduling rule, and its direction
is the point that is easy to get backwards. By \eqref{eq:angcrit} long segments
need many directions and short ones few, so the angular grid is doubled at the
\emph{finest} levels, in step with the doubling of $\ell_l$. While the
doubling is active,
\begin{equation}
  \delta\theta_{l-1}\,\ell_{l-1}
  = \frac{2\pi}{n_\theta^{\min}2^{\,l-1}}\cdot \ell_0 2^{\,l-1}
  = \frac{2\pi\ell_0}{n_\theta^{\min}} ,
  \label{eq:constant}
\end{equation}
independent of $l$: every refinement faces the same criterion, and with
$\ell_0\approx h$ the constant in \eqref{eq:angcrit} is
$c=2\pi/n_\theta^{\min}$. A single parameter, $n_\theta^{\min}$, controls the
angular interpolation error. Once \eqref{eq:schedule} saturates at $n_\theta$,
the remaining levels perform exact merges only and contribute no error at all.
Setting $n_\theta^{\min}=n_\theta$ disables every interpolation and recovers
direct evaluation, which is how the reference solutions in
Section~\ref{sec:results} are computed.

\subsection{Level transition}

Each level transition consists of an angular prolongation followed by a merge.

\paragraph{(A) Angular prolongation.}
$\Theta_{l-1}\subset\Theta_l$, so half the directions of $\Theta_l$ are copies
of level-$(l-1)$ directions and are taken over exactly. Each new direction
$\theta$ is the midpoint of two old ones, and its table is obtained by
interpolation in $\theta$ over a stencil of $q$ old directions. The
interpolation must be performed at a fixed \emph{spatial} point: the value of
the old table at direction $\theta'$ is needed at
$s'=\bx\cdot\omp(\theta')$, $t'=\bx\cdot\om(\theta')$ where
$\bx=s\,\omp(\theta)+t\,\om(\theta)$, which requires a two-dimensional
interpolation in the old frame's $(s,t)$ grid. Interpolating in $\theta$ at
fixed \emph{ray} coordinates instead would rotate the segment centre on a
circle of radius $|\bx|=\bigO(1)$ and, by \eqref{eq:sinc}, force
$\delta\theta\lesssim h$ at every level, destroying the complexity.

\paragraph{(B) Merge.}
At each direction, the level-$l$ segment centred at level-$(l-1)$ node index
$2j$ is the union of the level-$(l-1)$ segments centred at $2j\mp\nu/2$; the
offset $\ell_{l-1}/2$ is an integer number of grid steps precisely because
$\nu$ is even. The triples are combined by \eqref{eq:merge}. No interpolation
in $t$ occurs, and the step is exact.

\subsection{Finest level}

Level-0 segments have length $\approx h$ and are evaluated directly. Place $m$
Gauss--Legendre nodes $\{t_i\}$ with weights $\{w_i\}$ on the segment, sample
$f$ and $\mu$ there by tensor-product interpolation from the image grid, and
take
\begin{equation}
  E=\frac{\ell}{2}\sum_j w_j\mu_j,\qquad
  S=\frac{\ell}{2}\sum_j w_j f_j,\qquad
  A_i=\frac{\ell}{2}\sum_j C_{ij}\mu_j,\qquad
  I=\frac{\ell}{2}\sum_i w_i f_i e^{-A_i},
  \label{eq:level0}
\end{equation}
where $C_{ij}=\int_{t_i}^{b}L_j$ and $L_j$ is the Lagrange basis on the nodes.
$A_i$ approximates $\int_{t_i}^{b}\mu$ to order $m$ from the same $m$ samples,
so the partial attenuations needed by $I$ cost one $m\times m$ matrix
application per segment. Note that \eqref{eq:level0} is accurate only while
$\mu\ell$ is moderate --- an $8$-node rule applied to a whole chord with
$E=30$ has relative error $10^{-3}$ --- which is precisely why the direct
quadrature is confined to the shortest segments and everything above is the
exact recursion \eqref{eq:merge}.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Multilevel evaluation of \eqref{eq:three}}
\label{alg:main}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State build level-0 tables by \eqref{eq:level0} on $\Theta_0\times\{s\}\times\{t\}$
\For{$l=1,\dots,L$}
  \State \textbf{(A)} copy tables at $\Theta_{l-1}$; interpolate the remaining
         directions of $\Theta_l$ in $(\theta,s,t)$
  \State \textbf{(B)} merge node pairs $2j\mp\nu/2$ by \eqref{eq:merge}
\EndFor
\State \Return the level-$L$ table at $t=0$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Zero extension}

Outside the tables the triples are taken to be zero. This is exact rather than
an approximation: if $|s'|>R$ the whole line misses the support, and if
$|t'|>H\ge R+\ell/2$ the segment does. The interpolation stencils may therefore
straddle the table boundary without loss, provided $f$ and $\mu$ vanish
smoothly; a field with a jump violates the smoothness on which every
interpolation in the method depends, and the order degrades accordingly
(Section~\ref{sec:knobs}).

\subsection{Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

Work at level $l$ is proportional to the table size $|\Theta_l|\,n_s\,n_t^{(l)}$
with $n_t^{(l)}=\nu\,2^{\,L-l}+1$. Writing $M=2^L\approx 2H/h$ and
$l^{*}=\log_2(n_\theta/n_\theta^{\min})$,
\begin{equation}
  |\Theta_l|\,n_t^{(l)} \approx
  \begin{cases}
    n_\theta^{\min} \nu M, & l\le l^{*}\quad\text{(refining)},\\[2pt]
    n_\theta\,\nu M/2^{\,l}, & l>l^{*}\quad\text{(coasting)} .
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
The refining levels each cost the same and the coasting levels decay
geometrically, so
\begin{equation}
  W_{\mathrm{ML}}
  = \bigO\!\big(n_\theta^{\min}\,\nu\,M\,n_s\,(l^{*}+2)\big)
  = \bigO(N\log N),\qquad N=n^2\approx M n_s ,
  \label{eq:cost}
\end{equation}
against $W_{\mathrm{direct}}=\bigO(n_\theta n_s M)=\bigO(N^{3/2})$. The
constant per refining level is $q\,p_s\,p_t$ table lookups, where $p_s,p_t$ are
the stencil widths.

\section{Numerical results}
\label{sec:results}

All experiments use the reference implementation in
\texttt{tomogrid}: cubic ($4$-point) stencils throughout, $m=8$
Gauss--Legendre nodes per level-0 segment, $\nu=2$, $\sigma=2$,
$n_\theta^{\min}=16$ unless stated, $H=1$, images on $[-1,1]^2$. Directions are
uniform on $[0,2\pi)$; the full circle is required because \eqref{eq:art} is
not invariant under $\theta\mapsto\theta+\pi$, unlike $S$ and $E$.

Three errors are reported, all measured in the supremum norm over the sinogram
and normalised by $\max|S|$, which is the physically meaningful scale since $I$
itself underflows on heavily attenuated rays:
\begin{align*}
  e_{\mathrm{alg}}  &: \text{multilevel vs direct evaluation on the \emph{same} discretisation};\\
  e_{\mathrm{disc}} &: \text{direct evaluation vs the continuum};\\
  e_{\mathrm{tot}}  &: \text{multilevel vs the continuum}.
\end{align*}
Separating them matters: $e_{\mathrm{alg}}$ is what the multilevel schedule
costs, and it is the only quantity the algorithm controls.

\subsection{Verification}

The continuum reference is a dense trapezoidal evaluation of the analytic
phantom. On the $C^\infty$ compactly supported fields used here every
Euler--Maclaurin boundary term vanishes and the rule is spectrally accurate:
against the closed-form Radon transform of a Gaussian with uniform attenuation
it agrees to $2\times10^{-14}$, and doubling its sample count changes the
result by INPUT_REFDRIFT.

The exactness claims are verified independently. Proposition~\ref{prop:merge}
reproduces whole-segment triples from split ones to $10^{-13}$; the nested
$t$-grid bookkeeping of step (B) reproduces directly computed coarse-level
tables to $10^{-12}$ for $\nu\in\{2,4,8\}$; and the physical limits hold to
roundoff --- with $\mu\equiv0$ the computed $I$ equals $S$ to $10^{-12}$, and
when the attenuator lies entirely downstream of the source the SPECT model
collapses onto the PET model $e^{-E}S$ to $10^{-10}$, while for the reversed
direction $I=S$ to the same tolerance.

\subsection{Attenuation battery}
\label{sec:battery}

Table~\ref{tab:battery} reports $e_{\mathrm{alg}}$ over nine attenuation fields
crossed with four activity fields on a deliberately coarse $33^2$ grid with
$n_\theta^{\min}=8$, where the method is under maximum stress. The fields
include a uniform soft-edged disc, single and multiple blobs, a high-contrast
case, an anisotropic blob with large $|\nabla\mu|$ in one direction, a linear
gradient, a short-wavelength oscillatory field, and a case with chord
attenuation $E\approx 12$.

The error varies by a factor of six across the battery, and short-wavelength
$\mu$ is no worse than smooth $\mu$. The strongly attenuating case gives the
\emph{smallest} error in $I$, as predicted by Remark~\ref{rem:reference}: the
factor $e^{-E[c,b]}$ in \eqref{eq:merge} damps upstream error. The worst case
is the high-contrast field, consistent with the interpolation constant growing
with $|\nabla\mu|$.

Two observations. First, $e_{\mathrm{alg}}$ is a factor of six to twelve below
$e_{\mathrm{disc}}$ at every resolution, so the multilevel schedule is
not what limits the accuracy of the computed sinogram; the discretisation of
the image is. Second, both errors converge under refinement, which is the
property that makes the method usable: the multilevel error is not a fixed
tolerance but goes to zero with the mesh.

The observed orders are pre-asymptotic on this phantom, which contains a blob
of radius $0.22$ that is under-resolved at $n=33$. On a well-resolved smooth
phantom (a single $C^\infty$ bump of radius $0.7$) the discretisation error
converges at observed orders $3.03$, $3.42$, $3.53$ over the same grid
sequence, approaching the fourth order of the cubic image interpolant. The
residual defect is attributable to the $C^0$ kinks of that interpolant, which
the level-0 quadrature straddles: raising the interpolation stencils from
fourth to sixth order does not improve the rate, whereas a $C^2$ image
representation would be expected to.

The measured cost in Table~\ref{tab:refine} follows \eqref{eq:cost}. Direct

The stencil widths in the lower block of Table~\ref{tab:knobs} locate the error
budget, and the answer is not what the design would suggest. Narrowing the
angular stencil from fourth to second order costs a factor of $1.9$, whereas
narrowing the transverse and along-ray stencils costs factors of $14$ and
$6.5$; widening the angular stencil to sixth order gains $2.5\%$ against
$23\%$ for the transverse one. The dominant term is therefore not the
interpolation in $\theta$ --- the operation the method is built around --- but
the resampling of the tables in $(s,t)$ that accompanies it, a consequence of
storing the tables in ray-aligned coordinates rather than an intrinsic property
of the multilevel decomposition. A formulation that avoids the resampling, for
instance by carrying frame-covariant quantities, would improve on what is
reported here.

Finally, the smoothness assumption is not decorative. Replacing the smooth
activity by a discontinuous disc raises $e_{\mathrm{alg}}$ by a factor of
$3.5$ at the settings of Table~\ref{tab:battery} and, more tellingly, the
error then stops converging under refinement.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Three are worth stating precisely. (i) On rays with very large total
attenuation the true signal underflows, and what the method controls is a small
error \emph{absolute} to $\max|S|$, not a small relative error; carrying the
mean survival weight $W=I/S$ instead of $I$ would address this, at the cost of
an indeterminate form wherever a segment misses the support. (ii) The order is
capped near three by the $C^0$ image interpolant rather than by the multilevel
structure. (iii) Zero extension is exact only for compactly supported fields;
a Gaussian truncated at the domain boundary at the $10^{-4}$ level produces a
matching error floor.

\paragraph{Extensions.}
The three-dimensional case is not a difficulty of principle. Concatenation
\eqref{eq:merge} is dimension-independent; the direction grid becomes
two-dimensional on the sphere, so \eqref{eq:angcrit} becomes an area criterion
and the number of directions at level $l$ grows as $(\ell_l/h)^2$, while the
number of segment positions falls by the same factor, so the work per level is
again constant.

The adjoint $\Rmu^{*}$, needed by every method in
Section~\ref{sec:motivation}, is the transpose of Algorithm~\ref{alg:main}
step by step: the merge transposes to a scatter and the angular prolongation to
anterpolation, in the sense of \citep{brandtlubrecht1990}. The transpose has
the same cost, and implementing it is the immediate next step, as is the
nonlinear inversion --- the FAS/FMG treatment of \eqref{eq:inverse}, and of the
joint estimation of $(f,\mu)$ in which $\mu$ enters nonlinearly --- sketched in
Section~\ref{sec:motivation}.

\section{Conclusion}

The attenuated Radon transform admits an $\bigO(N\log N)$ multilevel
evaluation. The construction rests on an exact concatenation identity for the
triple $(S,E,I)$, on interpolating the exponent rather than its exponential and
the exit-referenced rather than the midpoint-referenced attenuated integral,
and on a level schedule that refines the angular grid at the finest levels
where segments are short. On a battery of attenuation fields the error the
schedule introduces stays an order of magnitude below the discretisation error
of the grid and converges with it, at a cost already several times below direct
evaluation at moderate resolution and improving as $\bigO(\sqrt{N}/\log N)$.
